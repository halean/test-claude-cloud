My son, 17, said: “I love Hội An, don't you.” during a Sunday lunch when we discussed
what we should do instead of staying home. The simple question triggered a long stream
of thoughts. My name is An, which came from Hội An. My son's name is An, which came
from me. My father was born in Hội An, my grandfather had a mansion in Hội An. A typical
summer holiday back in the 80s would inevitably include a two-day road trip to Hội An.
Arriving late at night, we would unpack, and then walked straight to the night food
market, and ate chicken porridge. It was so good for a ten year old: after a long day
of travelling, half carsick, half bored, the porridge went straight into blood stream.
And there was more chicken than at home, as things were cheaper in Hội An than in Hồ
Chí Minh City.

The next morning we would stop some walking vendor balancing their merchandise on two
sides of a bamboo pole on their shoulder for “bánh bèo”. “Bánh bèo” is a very good
trick, the same trick they are deploying in Michelin stars these days. The portion is
very small, contained in a bowl smaller than half a child's hand. But one never eats one bowl: the pride was with the stack
of empty bowls, who can eat the most. Small bowls, many of them, make less go for more.
Back then, labour was cheap and materials were not. Main ingredients are
steamed rice flour, some fried spring onion, finely shredded prawn, and the “secret
dipping sauce”, which probably was prawn powder plus the right amount of condiments. I
would then draw some water from the mansion's well using rope and scoop, flicking the
wrist so that the scoop turned and scooped water. Apparently, this is a life skill, one
that I have not used since. Then washing clothes, and free time to explore the mansion
and its garden.

Lunch would be cooked in the outhouse using firewood. There would be more fish than I
would like, but everybody else seemed to like fish. The afternoon was boring time, some
more exploring the garden, perhaps walking down to the bridge/temple, mostly waiting for
the sun to get less harsh to go to the beach. How bored was I: we would feed an ant
to a sandpit and watch the antlion larva coming up to grab the ant. It was very crude,
but back then, it was entertainment. The beach back then was so shallow and
smooth, that we would waddle out 100–200m. After the swim, time to be back for another
meal. Night treat would include “lục tàu xá”, again, from a vendor chanting their treats
while walking past our house.

The house was a big one, sitting at a corner right on the edge of the old town. Back
then, Hội An was just a sleepy town with nothing interesting happening. Due to some
family dispute, the house was sold, on the cheap. Now it is worth millions of pounds.

Then Hội An became a booming tourist town. My mother jointly built a mini hotel, and my
father was an incidental hotel manager. He loved and hated it in equal amount. He loved
talking to tourists in French and English. Back then, the backpackers were tough
bargainers: how much for a night? That would be USD15, sir. But we don't need aircon,
make it 12, and we have a deal. Stories like that stay with me. My father eventually reunited with somebody from
France who was in the same primary school. Imagine that. At another time, he translated
an urgent request from another French guy: a suit, in 3 hours. The village tailor said,
impossible, 4 hours maybe. But I have to go to Huế by then. No problem, the tailor said,
where would you stay, we send somebody to deliver. Of course, they negotiated the
transaction through my father. The French guy asked my father in private: could they
deliver. Of course they could, my father said, not telling the real reason: they did
not have anything else to do. Rumours had it that that was the founding transaction of
the fast tailored fashion of Hội An.

The joint venture did not work out, and our family broke the link with Hội An.

Now, taking my son to Hội An, I cannot help but feel strange. The tourist hotspot is
very much different from what it was in my childhood. The house is now hosting multiple
cafés, Bánh Mì Phượng, and the like. The lantern festival is a very recent one, no more
simple night food market, very crowded now. Of course my son loves the festive feeling
of the town, the fact that it is compact, walkable, with lots of things to see. As for
myself, I did not love Hội An when I was a child, because it was boring. I do not love
Hội An now, because I know a lot of things are just for show. And one day, in 2019, a
street seller told me: “Anh tránh ra tí để em bán cho Tây”, move away please so that I
can sell some trinkets to these foreigners.
My fault: I spoke Vietnamese with a Hội An accent, so not the kind of customer she
thought she could sell to.

So of course, I could not say I love Hội An. But deep down, I still care.
